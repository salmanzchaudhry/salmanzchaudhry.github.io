The Problem

Where Patient Access Was Breaking Down

When I started pulling the Epic EHR data, the picture that came back was a referral process that had grown into something nobody had fully mapped. Steps were duplicated. Authorizations were being tracked inconsistently across teams. Clinical staff were spending real time on follow-up work that should have been automated or eliminated entirely. Nobody was measuring it, so nobody knew how much it was actually costing in time and patient volume.

The goal was to get specific. Not just "the process is slow" but where exactly, by how much, and which changes would actually move the numbers.

Before — State of the Process
  • Referral tracking was fragmented across multiple Epic workflows
  • No standardized pre/post authorization measurement in place
  • Non-value-added manual steps delayed specialist access by days
  • No visibility into authorization conversion rates by referral type
  • Clinical staff spending excessive time on manual follow-up
After — Post-Implementation
  • Referral workflow standardized with defined Epic tracking steps
  • KPI dashboard tracking authorization rates week-over-week
  • Non-value-added steps eliminated via DMAIC root cause analysis
  • 200 additional authorizations processed per quarter
  • 60 labor hours per month recovered for direct patient care

Methodology

How I Worked Through It

I followed the DMAIC structure from the beginning. Before touching any data, I spent time with the clinical and administrative staff who actually lived inside the process every day. Those conversations shaped everything that came after. They flagged things the data alone would never have surfaced, and they helped me understand which metrics actually mattered to the people doing the work versus the ones that looked good in a report.

From there I pulled baseline metrics from Epic EHR and built the pre/post KPI framework that became the backbone of the analysis. The measurements tracked authorization conversion rates, processing time, and cost per workflow. Post-implementation, I ran the same checks at 30, 60, and 90 days to confirm the changes held.

Key Learnings

What I Took Away From This

The biggest thing this internship taught me is that the hardest part of healthcare analytics is not the querying. It is knowing which questions to ask before you write a single line of SQL. The Define and Measure phases felt slow at the time, but every conversation with clinical staff narrowed my focus in ways the data alone could not. I would have built the wrong dashboard if I had started with the data instead of the people.

The other thing that stuck with me was what it felt like when the analysis actually landed. The gap analysis was not just a report that got filed somewhere. It was the specific evidence that convinced leadership to approve the workflow changes. That is a different kind of outcome than producing a clean chart, and it is the kind of work I want to keep doing.
